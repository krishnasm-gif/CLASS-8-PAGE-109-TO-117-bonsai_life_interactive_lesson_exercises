"Bonsai Life" (బోన్సాయ్ బ్రతుకులు) — Made Super Easy!

Written by renowned feminist writer Abburi Chayadevi (Translated from Telugu by Alladi Uma & M. Sridhar). Explore how a tiny potted Bonsai tree represents an uneducated, dependent woman, while a tall Turayi (Flame/Gulmohar) tree represents an educated, independent woman who shelters others!

Part I Recap (Pages 107–109)

1. Characters: Ammalu (the narrator, an educated working woman living in New Delhi) and Akkayya (her elder sister, uneducated, married to a village farmer).

2. The Visit: Ammalu gets excited seeing a letter from Akkayya saying she and Baavagaru are visiting Delhi on their way to Kasi and Haridwar. Akkayya brings traditional Andhra food items (gongura, drumsticks, cucumber, appadams, vadiyams).

3. Contrast in Lives: Ammalu sometimes feels tired managing both office and housework, wishing to give up her job. But Akkayya reminds her how lucky she is to earn her own money and live with dignity, unlike a dependent housewife who must beg her husband even for a few paise worth of karivepaku (curry leaves). That is why Akkayya is determined to send her daughter to college!

తెలుగు సారాంశం (Part I): రచయిత్రి (అమ్మలు) ఢిల్లీలో ఉద్యోగం చేసే విద్యావంతురాలు. ఆమె అక్కయ్య 5వ తరగతి వరకే చదివి పల్లెటూరిలో గృహిణిగా ఉంటుంది. భర్తపై ఆర్థికంగా ఆధారపడే జీవితం తేలును చెప్పు కింద పెట్టినట్లు అణచివేతగా ఉంటుందని, తన కూతురిని తప్పకుండా కాలేజీకి పంపిస్తానని అక్కయ్య చెప్తుంది.
Part II Summary (Pages 110–112)

1. The Balcony & Bonsai: To divert Akkayya from sad memories of how their father (Nannagaru) stopped her studies, Ammalu takes her to the balcony. Akkayya is shocked to see turayi and pomegranate trees stunted inside small flowerpots.

2. Bonsai Art vs Confinement: Ammalu proudly explains the Japanese art of Bonsai (trimming branches and roots to keep giant trees tiny). Akkayya sighs that it is cruel to confine a tall tree to a small pot.

3. The Storm & Realization: A sudden dust storm and rain hit Delhi. Ammalu rushes to bring the delicate bonsai pots indoors. Looking out the window, Akkayya points to a huge Turayi tree standing strong in the storm and giving shelter to many people. She compares a housewife's life to a delicate, dependent Bonsai. Touched deeply, Ammalu feels the urge to free her bonsai trees!

తెలుగు సారాంశం (Part II): పూలకుండీలలో మరుగుజ్జుగా పెంచిన బోన్సాయ్ చెట్లను చూసి అక్కయ్య బాధపడుతుంది. తుఫాను, వర్షం వచ్చినప్పుడు బయట స్వేచ్ఛగా పెరిగిన తురాయి చెట్టు ఎందరికో నీడనిస్తుంటే, బోన్సాయ్ చెట్టు మాత్రం తనను తాను కూడా రక్షించుకోలేక ఆధారపడుతుంది. ఆడపిల్లలను చదివించకపోతే వారి బ్రతుకు కూడా బోన్సాయ్ చెట్టులాగే అవుతుందని అక్కయ్య చెప్పిన మాటలతో రచయిత్రి మనసు మారుతుంది.

Bonsai Life — Part II (Full Lesson with Interactive Meanings)

💡 Tip: Tap or click any highlighted word to see its meaning instantly!

From the very start Akkayya had been keen on studying. But Nannagaru didn’t educate her. Because she was not adept at oral arithmetic, Nannagaru had said, “Ah ‘she’s a girl, how will studies get into her head?” and had made her discontinue her lessons concentrating only on Annayya’s education. Because she was uneducated, she got married to a man from a village, had to look after the cattle, keep the stove clean, draw water from the well... Amma used to be very upset that Akkayya had to go through such drudgery. Realizing that Akkayya was upset thinking of the past, and in an attempt to divert her mind, I led her to the balcony saying, “Come, let’s go and sit outside.”

Looking at the plants in the flowerpots, Akkayya mentioned that all the cucumbers, drumsticks and gongura were from their own backyard. I asked her to send some gongura seeds the next time someone came this way…

“But, Ammalu, what’s this? Why have you planted the turayi and pomegranate trees in these flowerpots? See, how stunted they have become! If, like flower plants, you put these trees in pots instead of letting them grow freely in the backyard, how will they grow?” she asked, surprised, feeling sorry for the trees.

I burst into laughter. Akkayya was perplexed.

“I did it on purpose. It’s a special method. It’s called bonsai in Japan. You can grow even a huge banyan tree in a flowerpot. You can grow it even with its roots hanging down from the branches. You have no idea how beautiful a pomegranate tree looks when you keep cutting its branches, changing the pot now and then, trimming it into a small-sized tree and making it bear fruit! Do you know how carefully you have to tend this small tree? Bonsai is a great art,” I said.

But it seemed as if Akkayya didn’t appreciate what I said. “I don’t know. You have confined a turayi tree to a flowerpot when it could have grown to the height of a building,” she sighed.

Feeling disheartened at being unable to impress Akkayya with my bonsai, I collapsed weakly into a chair. I was most distressed – as if the entire art I had learnt had come to nought. It was like throwing perfume into ash. Suddenly a dust storm began to rage. The sand hit our faces harshly. I caught hold of Akkayya’s shoulder and dragged her into the room. Then I closed the doors and windows in a hurry.

Akkayya was stunned. “What’s all this? Everything was normal till now. Where did that dust and wind come from suddenly? You have tar roads too,” she said.

“This is how it is in the big city, my dear. Before we know what is happening, the storm brings all the sand from the Rajasthan desert and hits our face…” I had not completed my sentence when I could hear the rain beginning to fall. I opened the door and pulled the bonsai tree pots and flowerpots inside, under the canopy. Akkayya opened side window and looked at the streets to observe the weather in the Indian capital. “Look, Ammalu, look there,” she said. A new enthusiasm seemed to have crept into her voice. I looked eagerly through the window towards the street. I couldn’t understand what she meant. I looked at her and said, “What is it?”

“Look at that tree… look at how many people are standing under it without getting wet,” she said, as if it was something out of the ordinary. I saw nothing unusual in it. Realising that I had not understood what she was getting at, she said again, “Look how tall that turayi has grown. Out in the open, see how freely it has grown. However powerful the sand storm, it hasn’t bowed a little bit. Moreover, it has provided shelter to so many people, and is protecting them. Imagine how many would find respite from the hot sun under its shade!”

“What’s so surprising about that?” I asked.

“Not that it is surprising, Ammalu. Look at the bonsai you have tended so lovingly! It looks proper and sweet, like a housewife. But see how delicate it is. You have to tend it very carefully. It can’t even withstand a small dust storm or squall. When it is dependent on someone, how can it provide shelter to anyone? Isn’t it because of the difference in the way one brings up a boy and a girl, that a woman’s life is like that of a bonsai?”

My heart was touched by Akkayya’s words. Just as one frees a bird from a cage to let it fly, I felt the urge to free the bonsai trees from their flower pots.

About the Author — Abburi Chayadevi (అబ్బూరి ఛాయాదేవి)

Abburi Chayadevi (1933–2019) is a well-known feminist writer. She has written many short stories and essays. She was awarded the prestigious Central Sahitya Akademi Award in 2005 (for her work Tana Margam). In her works, she elucidates women’s life and their feelings. (Original Telugu Title: "Bonsai Bratukulu", Translated by Alladi Uma and M. Sridhar).

II. Answer the following questions (Easy Student Notes + Practice)

Write your own answer or view/listen to the easy-to-learn model answers below.

1. “The moment I see letters….. can’t contain my excitement.” Why does the narrator get excited?

Model Answer: The narrator gets excited when she sees letters because she feels as if she is face-to-face with her near and dear ones and that they are speaking affectionately to her. The sight of a letter in a familiar handwriting immediately removes all her exhaustion from office work and fills her heart with joy and energy.

2. What change did the narrator observe in her father’s attitude towards education over a decade?

Model Answer: When Akkayya was young, their father (Nannagaru) believed girls did not need education beyond Class Five, thinking it was enough if a girl could keep the washerman's accounts. However, a decade later when the narrator was born, her father changed with the times, realized the importance of girls' education, and sent her to college without any hesitation.

3. Why was Akkayya determined to send her daughter to college?

Model Answer: Akkayya suffered a lot because she was uneducated and had to depend on her husband even for a few paise. She realized that without a college degree, a woman would come to nothing and would have to live suppressed under her husband’s thumb "like a scorpion under a slipper." Therefore, she was determined to educate her daughter so she could stand on her own feet.

4. In what way is the narrator more fortunate than her sister?

Model Answer: The narrator is more fortunate than her sister because her father sent her to college and gave her higher education. Because of her education, she secured a good job in the city, earns well, and leads an independent, dignified life without having to beg anyone for money.

5. Can one be independent without a job or earning? Justify your answer.

Model Answer: No, it is very difficult to be truly independent without a job or earning. Financial independence gives a person self-confidence, decision-making power, and dignity. Without an income, one has to depend on others even for basic daily needs, just as Akkayya had to depend on her husband even for a few paise worth of curry leaves.

II. Answer the following questions (Part II)

1. Why was Akkayya perplexed?

Answer: Akkayya was perplexed (confused) because when she expressed pity for the stunted turayi and pomegranate trees planted in small flowerpots, the narrator (Ammalu) burst into laughter instead of feeling sorry for the trees.

2. How is a bonsai reared?

Answer: A bonsai is reared by planting a tree in a small flowerpot, continuously cutting and trimming its branches and roots, changing the pot now and then, and tending it very carefully so that it remains a tiny, miniature tree that still bears flowers or fruit.

3. What similarities do you notice between the bonsai tree and the housewife?

Answer: Both the bonsai tree and an uneducated housewife look sweet, proper, and delicate, but both are confined to a small space (a flowerpot / the four walls of a house). Neither is allowed to grow to their full natural potential. Both are completely dependent on others for survival and protection, and neither can withstand a sudden storm/crisis on their own or provide shelter to others.

4. What made the narrator feel the urge to free the bonsai?

Answer: During the rain and dust storm, Akkayya showed the narrator how a free-grown turayi tree stood tall against the storm and sheltered many people, whereas the bonsai had to be protected indoors. When Akkayya compared the stunted, dependent bonsai to a woman's restricted life, the narrator's heart was deeply touched and she felt the urge to free the bonsai trees from their flowerpots just like freeing a caged bird.

5. What is the central theme of ‘Bonsai Life’?

Answer: The central theme of ‘Bonsai Life’ is Women Empowerment through Education and Financial Independence. The story highlights that girls should not be confined and stunted like bonsai plants; instead, they must be educated and allowed to grow freely like tall trees so they can live with dignity, face life’s storms, and support others.

III. Make a list of activities done by a Homemaker and a Working Woman

Homemaker (గృహిణి చేసే పనులు)
  • Cleaning the house, sweeping, and mopping.
  • Keeping the stove and kitchen clean; drawing/fetching water.

Working Woman (ఉద్యోగిని చేసే పనులు)
  • Cooking quick meals, packing lunch boxes, and getting children ready in the morning.
  • Commuting to the workplace and attending office duties on time.
  • Working on files, attending meetings, and solving professional problems.
  • Earning a salary, managing bank accounts, and supporting the family financially.
  • Buying groceries and essentials on the way back home.
  • Returning home in the evening to prepare coffee/dinner and help kids with homework.

I. Phrasal Verbs beginning with ‘give’ and ‘look’

A phrasal verb (Verb + Preposition/Adverb) gives a special meaning different from its individual parts. E.g., ‘give up’ = to stop doing something.

1. give in Meaning: yield / surrender / accept defeat

Own Sentence: The freedom fighters refused to give in to the Britishers.

2. give out Meaning: distribute / emit / come to an end

Own Sentence: The teacher asked the monitor to give out the question papers to the students.

3. give away Meaning: distribute prizes / donate freely

Own Sentence: The Chief Guest was invited to give away the prizes on Annual Day.

4. look after Meaning: take care of

Own Sentence: We must lovingly look after our parents and grandparents.

5. look up Meaning: search for a word/fact in a dictionary

Own Sentence: Please look up the meaning of this difficult word in the dictionary.

6. look into Meaning: examine / investigate carefully

Own Sentence: The Principal promised to look into the students' request immediately.

Listening: Debate — “Education of the Girl Child Is a Burden”

Speaker 1 (For the motion - 'Is a Burden'): Respected Chairperson, honourable Judges and dear friends, I stand here to express my views for the motion, ‘Education of the girl child is a burden’. In a poor family the main concern for the head of the family is to provide food, clothing and health to all the members. Most of their resources are used for these priorities. Later, when they think of education, the first preference goes to the male child as he would be growing to be the breadwinner of the family, whereas the girl would leave the family one day. So, I feel that educating the girl child is a burden.

Speaker 2 (Against the motion - 'Not a Burden'): Respected chairperson, honourable Judges and dear friends! My knowledgeable opponent is of the opinion that the education of the girl child is a burden. May I ask how education of the girl could be a burden when she is an equal partner in sharing the responsibilities and duties? If given a chance, she will be sharing the burden of the family at least till she gets married. So, I strongly oppose the motion.

Speaker 1: When my opponent feels that the girl child would leave the family one day after marriage, can’t we agree that it is a waste of money to educate a girl child? Instead, the families can save the money to bear the expenses of her wedding. Yes, surely the girl can reduce the burden not by earning after education but by managing the household work. As her duty in the later part of life is to look after the family and children, she better gets practice in the same. If she is away from home for longer periods, it would be an additional burden on the family.

Speaker 2: My friend said, the future of the girl child is to look after the family and children. Haven’t such traditional gender roles led to inequalities in the society? I strongly feel that an educated girl can render financial assistance to the father and later to the husband. My dear friend, it is education that will bring about a change in the attitude of people towards the role of women. Indeed, it is rightly said: ‘If you educate a man, you educate an individual. If you educate a woman, you educate a family’.

Speaker Arguments FOR ('Education of Girl Child is a Burden') Arguments AGAINST ('Education of Girl Child is NOT a Burden')
Speaker 1 1. In poor families, priority goes to food, clothing, and educating the boy (future breadwinner), while a girl leaves the family after marriage. —
Speaker 2 — 1. A girl is an equal partner in sharing responsibilities and duties; if educated, she shares the burden of the family.
Speaker 1 2. Money spent on a girl's education can instead be saved for her wedding expenses. —
Speaker 2 — 2. Confining girls to housework creates gender inequality; an educated girl gives financial help to both her father and later her husband.
Speaker 1 3. A girl's future duty is managing household work and children, so staying away for studies is an extra burden. —
Speaker 2 — 3. Education changes society's attitude towards women: "If you educate a man, you educate an individual. If you educate a woman, you educate a family."
